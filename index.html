02 / THE RESTROOM

The actual simulator. You can change the layout and watch it run.

The project I really wanted you to see.

Why is the women’s
line always longer?

I wanted to understand how the way we allocate restroom space affects who ends up waiting. So I built a simulator to try different layouts.

You can change the arrival rate, watch people move through the space, and compare the tradeoff between serving more people and making the waits more equal.

I’m proud of this one because you don’t have to read my code to explore the question. You can try it yourself.

Can my menstrual cycle predict the stock cycle?

A surprising question, with a useful negative result. I classified 15,000+ health observations with F1 above 0.80, but Dynamic Time Warping found no evidence of menstrual–market alignment.

PRICES & GENDER

The pink tax wasn’t one simple number.

I scraped 100,000 product observations across three marketplaces in India and Japan. The mean premium was +12.9%, but the median was zero; women paid more in fewer than 40% of matched pairs.

CUSTOMER ORDERS
5,000 source orders
400 customer records
22 data tests

A pipeline for customer orders

I built a dbt and DuckDB pipeline that combines orders and payments without counting an order twice. Dagster handles the scheduled runs and retries.

The incremental output matched a full refresh. The useful surprise: on this small dataset, incremental processing wasn’t faster. Correctness and speed needed separate checks.

Before137.68 ms
After17.80 ms

120,000 pageviews · 3 measured runs
DuckDB 1.4.5 · one thread

Rewriting two slow queries

I replaced expensive joins with window functions and grouped aggregation. Then I compared the answers and tested the awkward cases, including timestamp ties and session boundaries.

The session query ran about 7.7× faster in the saved local benchmark. The case study includes the query plans, raw timings, and the limits of that measurement.

Does another step actually help?

I compared retrieval configurations with the same generator and prompt. Adding a reranker improved one setup but made another worse.

Looking at the failed answers was more useful than declaring a winner. In one case, the right passage was already there—the answer still chose the wrong plan.
